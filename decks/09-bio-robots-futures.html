the wet lab still has a veto

Five documented limits — one of them is a Nobel-team-sized surprise

  • Disordered and low-confidence regions are common — the model is honest about it via pLDDT, and biology lives there
  • Complexes are harder than chains: AF-Multimer was accurate for protein–protein interactions roughly 70% of the time (2021)
  • Off-distribution ligands: AF3 accuracy "drops markedly" outside its training distribution, and it is insensitive to adversarial decoys — signs of memorization
  • Glycans: 50–70% of human-proteome structures would be incomplete without them
  • Orphan and synthetic proteins have no multiple-sequence alignment, so the evolutionary signal the model leans on isn't there
  • And even with perfect static structures: folding pathways and dynamics remain unsolved

The surprise

At CASP16 (Nov 2024), on real pharmaceutical targets, AF3-based models did not significantly beat older methods at pose prediction. The headline benchmark and the use case a pharma team cares about are not the same measurement.

Reported in July 2026: DeepMind disbanded the Nobel-winning AlphaFold team. A public good can still be abandoned by a corporate strategy — access is not stewardship.
Limits and quotes via the AlphaFold article (multimer ~70%, AF3 off-distribution drop, decoy insensitivity/memorization, 50–70% glycan incompleteness, orphan proteins, dynamics); CASP16 pharma-target result via the Isomorphic article (Nov 2024); team dissolution attributed to FT reporting, cited there. Say "reportedly" for the team — it's a news attribution, not a primary source.
one drug, and the base rates around it

An AI-designed drug reached Phase 2a. That is the win. Here is the denominator.

Rentosertib (ISM3312), Insilico

  • AI-identified TNIK inhibitor for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis
  • Peer-reviewed Phase I: 78 healthy volunteers (NCT05154240) + a separate China Phase I, then a randomized Phase 2a
  • Company framing: target → clinical candidate in 18 months (press, not audited)
  • Insilico listed in Hong Kong late 2025, raising ~$293M

The denominator

  • Wong/Siah/Lo (Biostatistics 2019): 406,038 trial entries, 21,143 compounds, 2000–2015
  • Oncology success rate 3.4% (lower than the 5.1% often quoted)
  • Trials using biomarkers for patient selection succeed more — so the compressible part is selection and design, not magic
  • The "$2.6M discovery cost" figure is everywhere and does not verify — don't use it
Self-driving labs are real too: Coscientist (Nature 2023) planned and ran chemistry experiments on a cloud lab autonomously; GNoME proposed 2.2M candidate crystals, 736 already independently synthesized.
Rentosertib: Nat Biotechnol 2025, PMID 38459338; trial count and randomisation from the paper's reporting. Base rates: PMID 29394327. Coscientist: PMID 38123806. GNoME: Nature 2023. A pharma lead says: "AlphaFold 3 predicts binding affinity, so it replaces our lead-optimisation chemistry." Correct response?
AF3 does model ligand-containing complexes — but structure ≠ affinity. Affinity prediction is the (vendor-claimed) territory of Boltz-2, "FEP-class at ~1000× faster". And the CASP16 pharma-target result is the honest caveat. Three different claims, one sentence apart.
This is the most common conflation in the room. Reinforce: structure prediction (Nobel), affinity (vendor claim, not independently replicated in my sources), and the wet-lab confirmation loop that neither replaces.

The data wall, in numbers

DROID: 76k trajectories · 350 hours · 564 scenes · 50 collectors · 3 continents · 12 months. Open X-Embodiment: 22 robots · 21 institutions · 160,266 tasks. Teleop hours are the labelled data; simulation is the attempted shortcut — nothing published proves sim alone closes the contact-rich gap.

What imitation learning already does

ACT / ALOHA (2023): 80–90% success on fine bimanual tasks from 10 minutes of demonstrations. Mobile ALOHA (2024): 50 demos + co-training lifts success by up to 90% relative. This is the cheapest capability gain in robotics today.

Sources: RT-2 (arXiv:2307.15818, 6k eval trials), PaLM-E (562B), π0 (RSS 2025) and π0.5 (arXiv:2504.16054), Gemini Robotics and 1.5/ER 1.5 (Oct 2025, ~100 demos), Figure Helix (figure.ai: System1/System2, onboard embedded GPUs, ~500 h, "<5%" of prior dataset sizes), GR00T N1 (arXiv:2503.14734) + Isaac Lab / Cosmos READMEs, DROID and Open X-Embodiment abstracts, ACT/ALOHA and Mobile ALOHA abstracts.
what "90% success" means

Dexterity works in the lab; autonomy lasts minutes in the wild. The hardware is already cheap.

Read the number before you buy

  • 80–90% is per task, per lab, curated setup — the same object, the same table, the same lighting
  • There is no home-robot equivalent of a multi-camera standard eval; nobody can yet rank two robots on "does the dishes anywhere"
  • π0.5's own claim is generalisation to cleaning tasks in unseen homes — not autonomous living
  • Fragility is measured in minutes to hours: recovery from a bad grasp, a blocked wrist, a confusables drawer
$16k
Unitree G1 humanoid at launch, Aug 2024
$13.5k
the same class of robot, listed "from" today — hardware is commoditising faster than skills
The implication for a company: the robot is now a peripheral. The scarce assets are demonstration data, task definition, and a maintenance contract. Buy the skills, not the chassis.
Success-rate caveats: pi05_abs.html (their own framing), ACT/Mobile ALOHA abstracts. Prices: wikipedia_unitree.html (G1 ~US$16,000, Aug 2024) and the G1 page currently showing "Price from $13.5K". If someone asks about Figure's humanoid pricing: no public price — that is itself an answer about who the customer is.
A vendor shows a humanoid folding laundry at 90% success and asks for a pilot. What does that number license you to conclude?
Both extremes are wrong: the number is real and reproducible in its setup, and it says nothing about your rooms. The right ask is a pilot in your environment, on your tasks, with a success definition written down first.
This generalises beyond robotics — the same discipline applies to any vendor accuracy claim: define the task distribution before accepting a percentage.

the soft kill: spoofing

Before you outsource autonomy, know that position itself can be forged

  • 2011: a US RQ-170 was captured — assessment: likely by GPS spoofing, not a hack of the aircraft
  • 2017: ~20 ships in the Black Sea displayed positions placing them at an airport; traced to GPS interference (Humphreys' analysis)
  • By 2026: Lloyd's List reporting of intense GNSS spoofing around the Black Sea, Sea of Azov, Baltic and Red Sea — now a routine hazard for civilian shipping
  • The detectors are attackable too: adversarial RF attacks against RF-based drone detection (arXiv 2512.20712); adversarial patches defeat aerial visual detection (arXiv 2008.13671)

Engineering consequences

  • Position is not a trusted input. Multi-source PNT + consistency checks, or you inherit someone else's map
  • GPS-denied autonomy (LiDAR/vision SLAM) is a security feature, not a demo feature
  • Lost-link behaviour is a policy: what may it still do, for how long, toward what?
  • Every perception model you depend on can be adversarially patched — assume detection recall degrades under opposition
Spoofing incidents and dates via the GPS-spoofing article (RQ-170, 2017 Black Sea with Todd Humphreys' analysis, 2026 Lloyd's List reporting); adversarial attacks via arXiv:2512.20712 and arXiv:2008.13671; GPS-denied navigation survey arXiv:2509.10730. Same argument as the guardrails deck: the identity of your inputs is part of your threat model — here the input is "where am I".
the contested kill chain

The evidence that autonomous drones have already killed is genuinely disputed — and that is the lesson

  • 24 Aug 2026, NYT: "A Drone Killed Three Ukrainians. It Was Guided by A.I."
  • June 2026, New Scientist (citing a Ukrainian defense-industry figure): fully autonomous "Terminator" drones killed soldiers around 2024 — reportedly a first
  • May 2026, Al Jazeera counters that humans remain in control of current operations
  • Drones are credited with roughly 70% of casualties in that war — a single attribution, so treat it as an order of magnitude

Two facts, one gap

Deployment: terminal autonomy after radio loss is documented and spreading.
Governance: UN Secretary-General and the ICRC called for binding treaty negotiations to conclude by end-2026; the UNGA voted 166–3–15 in Dec 2024; the CCW process has been meeting since 2014 without a mandate.

Capability is outrunning the negotiating room. Say the disputed part out loud: we cannot verify the first autonomous kill, and we should not pretend we can.
All four items come from the lethal-autonomy article's sourcing (NYT 2026-08-24; New Scientist June 2026; Al Jazeera May 2026; the ~70% casualty figure flagged as single attribution in my claim audit). UN/ICRC and the vote via the killer-robots and CCW articles. Present this as contested evidence, not a settled fact — that is the honest version and it is more persuasive.

live demo · the futures dial

Five levers. Toggle them and watch which future you are actually buying.

How to drive it: start at "nothing deployed" — the dystopia bar is high and the utopia bar is only modestly high, because ungoverned capability still does good things and the harms are what get scaled. Turn on screening and dual-use review: bio harm falls, research speed falls a little (the 23–32% floodlight is the thing you're slowing). Add tiered access: CBRN-ish risk falls further, but note the cost — access rationing is itself a concentration risk (the June 2026 vetted-partner episode). Turn on doctrine: swarm/robot incident risk drops sharply and cheaply — the cheapest lever on the panel, which is an argument. Turn on provenance/labelling: epistemic harm falls but only if enforcement exists. Finally "distributed ownership" is the one that raises utopia rather than lowering dystopia. Close with: the dial is a scenario model, not a forecast — every weight is a judgment, the levers and their mechanisms are sourced.

What decides which one

Three jurisdictions, and a checklist you can start on Monday.

Section 5: the regulatory map, the org-level checklist, the last quiz, and the recap.
three jurisdictions, three styles

Statute, deal, and licence — and a checklist that works under all three

EU — statute

  • AI Act in force 1 Aug 2024; prohibitions (incl. social-scoring recital) from 2 Feb 2025
  • GPAI obligations 2 Aug 2025; high-risk + Article 50 transparency 2 Aug 2026/27
  • Fines up to €35M / 7% of turnover
  • Known weakness: heavy provider self-assessment, few third-party audits

US — deal

  • "Restoring Gold Standard Science" EO (May 2025) and the Genesis Mission (24 Nov 2025): DOE AI-for-science, Argonne hosting two AI supercomputers with NVIDIA and Oracle
  • Government-lab evaluation MOUs (2024) and direct model access controls (2026)
  • Governs by contract and access, not statute

China — licence

  • CAC Interim Measures for Generative AI (Jul 2023)
  • Deep-synthesis labelling rules effective Jan 2023
  • 117 models approved by Apr 2024; training-data safety documentation (2025)
  • With US chip export controls cutting the other way since Oct 2022
Your five, regardless of jurisdiction: tiered model access · pre-release capability evals against a public rubric · order screening + incident logging · a written lost-link / engagement doctrine before any autonomous hardware moves · provenance on generated content. Each is mirrored by practice already on the record.

recap

Five things to remember

  • Prediction is free; the wet lab is not. 200M structures and a 23–32% research floodlight — and design still needs 10,000 candidates to find a hit.
  • Robotics' bottleneck is demonstration hours, not the network: 10 minutes of teleop already buys 80–90% on a narrow task, and a humanoid costs less than a car.
  • In a swarm, AI is at mission level, not the servo — and the fast failure modes are forged position and pre-baked lost-link autonomy.
  • Both futures are already running, with citations. COMPAS and GraphCast are the same kind of artifact: powerful, measured, and distributed unequally.
  • The controls are the story. Screening, tiers, doctrine, provenance, ownership — capability curves don't choose; procurement and policy do.
